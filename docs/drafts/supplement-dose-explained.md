# What the Dose on the Front of the Pack Means

**SEO title:** What the Dose on a Supplement Pack Means (UK Guide)
**Meta description:** What the dose figure on a supplement means: mg, mcg and IU units, NRV percentages, per-serving amounts, and why a bigger number is not automatically better.

The big number on the front of a supplement, the milligrams or micrograms next to the ingredient name, tells you the amount of that ingredient per serving. That is all it denotes: a quantity. It does not tell you what the ingredient does, whether that amount is right for you, or whether a higher number is better. This guide explains what the dose figure means, the units it comes in, how it relates to the reference amounts you see as percentages, and how to read it without being misled by a large number. For whether a given amount suits you, your health or your medication, that is a question for a pharmacist or doctor, not a label.

## What the number actually is

The dose on the front is a measurement of quantity, expressed per serving. If a pack states a collagen amount in grams, or a biotin amount in micrograms, that is how much of the ingredient one serving contains. The key phrase, again, is per serving, not per pack and not per unit unless the serving is a single unit. A large front-of-pack figure with a two-unit serving means each unit carries half of it, which matters when you compare against a product with a one-unit serving.

## The units: mg, mcg, µg, g, IU

Supplements use a handful of units and they are not interchangeable, so it helps to know them.

A gram, g, is the largest of the common units and turns up for ingredients taken in bulk, such as collagen. A milligram, mg, is a thousandth of a gram. A microgram, written mcg or µg, is a thousandth of a milligram, and appears for ingredients needed in tiny amounts, such as biotin. The symbols mcg and µg mean the same thing. You will occasionally see IU, international units, an older measure still used for certain vitamins, which does not convert simply to a weight because it depends on the specific substance.

The practical point is to check the unit before comparing two numbers. A figure in micrograms and a figure in milligrams are a thousandfold apart, so comparing the digits alone without reading the unit is a genuine and common error.

## The percentage next to it: NRV

Many ingredients show a percentage alongside the amount. In the UK this is usually the percentage of the Nutrient Reference Value, or NRV, a standard daily reference figure used on labels. A serving providing 100 percent NRV of a nutrient supplies the full reference amount. A figure above 100 percent means the serving provides more than the reference amount.

The NRV is a labelling reference, not a personal recommendation, and not every ingredient has one. Many popular beauty supplement ingredients, including collagen and various botanical extracts, have no NRV, so they show an amount with no percentage. That absence is normal and does not indicate anything is missing from the label.

## Why a bigger number is not automatically better

It is tempting to read a larger dose as a better product, and the label design often encourages that. It is not a safe assumption. A higher amount per serving is simply a higher quantity; whether more is useful, unnecessary or something to be cautious about depends entirely on the ingredient and on you, which is exactly the territory a pharmacist or doctor covers and a label cannot. Some ingredients have upper levels above which more is pointless or inadvisable. This is one more reason the front-of-pack number should be read as information, not as a score to be maximised.

For comparison purposes, the dose figure is genuinely useful in one specific way: it lets you compare like with like. If you are choosing between two products of the same ingredient, knowing the amount per serving, in the same unit, lets you compare them on a fair footing, ideally alongside the cost per serving so you are weighing both content and value together. The [guide to comparing per serving](/articles/supplement-price-per-serving.html) covers how to bring those two together.

## Putting the dose in context of the whole label

The dose is one figure among several that together tell you what you are buying. To read it properly you also want the serving size and the servings per container, so you know how much you are getting each time and how long the pack lasts. All three sit in the information panel, and the [guide to reading a supplement label](/articles/how-to-read-supplement-label.html) walks through how they fit together. The dose tells you the amount per serving; the serving size and serving count tell you the rest.
