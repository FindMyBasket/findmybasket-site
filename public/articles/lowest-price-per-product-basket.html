Why the Lowest Price Per Product Can Cost You More

By FindMyBasket  ·  Updated August 2026  ·  6 min read

If you buy each product in your routine from wherever it happens to be at its best price, you will very often pay more overall than if you had bought the whole lot from one or two well-chosen retailers. It sounds backwards, but the reason is simple: chasing the best price on each individual item ignores delivery, and delivery is where the maths quietly turns against you. Once you add up separate postage charges across three or four different shops, the few pounds you saved per product can vanish, and then some.

This is the single most useful thing to understand about buying a beauty routine, and almost nobody shopping product by product realises it is happening. Here is exactly how it works and how to avoid it.

The trap: three best prices, three delivery charges

Picture a five-product routine: a cleanser, a serum, a moisturiser, an SPF and a treatment. You do your research and find that each product is at its best price at a different retailer. Naturally, you buy each one where it costs least. Five products, and let's say they land across three different shops.

You have now paid three separate delivery charges. Each one on its own feels minor, but together they can easily add up to more than the total you saved by splitting your order across those shops. You optimised each product and pessimised the basket. The individual wins were real, but the thing you actually pay for is the basket, delivered, and that number went up.

This is not a rare edge case. It is the default outcome of per-product shopping, because the retailer with the best price on your cleanser is usually not the one with the best price on your serum, which is usually not the one with the best price on your SPF. The best-price-per-item strategy almost guarantees a split order, and a split order almost guarantees stacked delivery.

Delivery thresholds make it worse, and they can also fix it

Most UK beauty retailers offer free delivery once your order passes a spend threshold. That threshold is the hinge the whole thing turns on.

When you split a routine across shops to chase per-item prices, you often land just under the free-delivery threshold at each one. You end up paying to post several small orders, none of which was big enough to qualify for free delivery on its own. But if you had consolidated more of your routine at a single retailer, that same spend might have cleared the threshold, and the delivery charge would have disappeared entirely.

This is the counterintuitive bit. Sometimes the best-value basket includes a product that is not at its individual best price, because putting it in the same order as the rest tips you over a free-delivery threshold and saves more than the small premium on that one item. A slightly higher unit price can be the better choice once delivery is in the picture. Per-product comparison can never see this, because it looks at one product at a time and never at the shape of the whole order.

Why single-product comparison tools miss this entirely

Almost every price comparison tool in the beauty space answers one question: where is this one product at its best price? That is a genuinely useful question, and for a single purchase it is the right one. The problem is that a routine is not a single purchase. It is a basket, and a basket has properties that no individual product has: a total, a delivery cost that depends on how the items are grouped, and thresholds that reward consolidation.

Optimising each product independently and then summing the results is not the same as optimising the basket. In fact the two answers routinely disagree, and the basket answer is almost always the one that costs less once delivery is counted. A tool that compares products one at a time is answering a smaller question than the one you actually have.

What "best value" really means for a routine

The number that matters is not the best price on any single item. It is the lowest total cost to get your whole routine delivered to your door. Reaching it means weighing up several things at once:

Which retailers stock which of your products, since you can only consolidate where a shop actually carries the item. What each product costs at each of those retailers, including any current offers. What each retailer charges for delivery, and at what spend the charge drops to zero. And then the combination: the split across one or two retailers that produces the lowest delivered total.

Sometimes the answer is buy everything from one shop. Sometimes it is buy most of it from one shop and one item from another. It genuinely varies by routine, by retailer roster and by the offers running that week, which is exactly why it is hard to work out in your head and easy to get wrong by shopping item by item.

How to actually do this without a spreadsheet

You could work all this out manually. List your products, open a dozen tabs, note each price, note each delivery charge and threshold, then try every combination of shops to find the lowest delivered total. It is doable. The bottom line

The best price on a product is not the same as the best value for your routine, and the gap between them is delivery. Shopping item by item scatters your order across shops, stacks up postage and often lands you just short of the free-delivery thresholds that would have saved you the most. The fix is to stop pricing products and start pricing the basket. Decide what you want to buy, then compare the whole routine, delivered, across UK retailers. That total, the one that includes getting it to your door, is the only one worth optimising.
