Why Supplement Prices Are Hard to Compare, and How to Do It Per Serving

By FindMyBasket  ·  Updated August 2026  ·  7 min read

Two supplements can look like they should cost the same and be wildly different value, because the price on the pack tells you almost nothing on its own. A supplement's real unit is the serving, and packs vary in how many servings they contain, how many units make up a serving, and how much of the active ingredient each serving delivers. That means a 60-count pack and a 120-count pack are often not comparable at all, and comparing their sticker prices is meaningless. The way to compare supplements properly is per serving. Here is why the sticker price misleads, and how to do the comparison that actually reflects value.

Why the pack price tells you so little

With most products, price roughly tracks what you get. With supplements, three separate variables sit between the price and the value, and any one of them can flip which product is better value.

The first is serving count. A pack states a number of units, but the number of servings depends on the serving size. A pack of 120 capsules with a two-capsule serving is a 60-serving pack. A pack of 60 capsules with a one-capsule serving is also a 60-serving pack. The unit counts differ by double, but the number of servings is identical. Compare on unit count and you draw the wrong conclusion immediately.

The second is serving size. As above, some products are one unit per serving and others are two, three or a scoop. The directions on the pack define this, and it changes how long a pack lasts and therefore what each day costs.

The third is strength per serving. Two products can both be, say, a collagen supplement, but deliver different amounts per serving. A pack that costs more but delivers more per serving, or more servings overall, can be better value than one with a lower sticker price. Sticker price is doing no useful work here, because it refers to the pack, and the pack is not the unit you are buying.

The fix: price per serving

The way through all of this is one figure: price per serving. Divide the pack price by the number of servings the pack contains, and you have a number you can compare across products regardless of pack size, serving size or count.

To work it out you need two things from the label, both covered in the guide to reading a supplement label: the servings per container, and the pack price. Price divided by servings gives cost per serving. If you want to be more precise for products with different strengths, you can go a step further and look at cost per serving alongside the amount of the active per serving, so you are comparing like with like on both cost and content.

This is the calculation that turns an impossible-looking comparison into a simple one. A larger pack often has a lower price per serving even though its sticker price is higher, because the cost is spread over more servings. Sometimes the smaller pack wins because of an offer. You cannot tell without doing the per-serving maths, and almost nobody does it standing in a shop or scrolling a product page.

A worked way of thinking about it

You do not need real prices to see the logic. Imagine two collagen products. One is a 30-serving pack, the other a 60-serving pack at a higher sticker price. If the 60-serving pack's price is less than double the 30-serving pack's, it is better value per serving, because you are getting twice the servings for less than twice the price. If it is more than double, the smaller pack wins. The sticker price alone would have told you the smaller pack cost less and left you worse off. Per serving is the only lens that gets this right.

The same applies across formats. A powder and a capsule version of a similar supplement can only be compared sensibly per serving, because the number of units means completely different things in each format. The guide to formats covers this in more detail.

Why this is exactly the problem whole-basket comparison solves

There is a second layer that makes supplement comparison even harder, and it is the same issue that affects any beauty purchase: delivery. If you buy your collagen from one retailer and your biotin from another because each looked better value in isolation, you can end up paying two delivery charges and landing under two free-delivery thresholds, which erases the per-serving saving you carefully worked out. This is the same trap covered in the article on why the lowest price per product can cost you more: the individual best price is not the same as the best-value delivered basket.

So the full picture for supplements is: compare per serving to get the true value of each product, then compare the whole basket across retailers to account for delivery. Doing only the first still leaves money on the table at the checkout.
